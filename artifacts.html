Overview

ForaU was an initiative developed to explore how Fora could expand its travel advisor model into the college student market. The project began with a core question: could college students be a new generation of travel advisors, and what would Fora need to build to effectively reach and serve them?

I approached ForaU as a zero-to-one product and growth initiative. My work spanned hypothesizing a consumer gap, validating market fit, and building out the product strategy. It became more than just adapting Fora's current offering for a younger audience. It meant understanding the motivations and barriers of college students, then using that information to design an experience for them.

The Use Case

College students are highly engaged with travel and frequently influence the travel decisions of their friends, families, and social networks. However, traditional travel advising is rarely positioned as an accessible entrepreneurial opportunity for students. To our generation, a "travel agent" feels outdated or overly excessive.

This made me think about a new demographic for Fora, one that could give students a way to turn their existing interest in travel and personal networks into an entrepreneurial experience.

The Hypothesis

If Fora could reduce the barriers to becoming a travel advisor and position the opportunity in a way that resonated with students, college campuses could become a scalable acquisition channel for the next generation of Fora advisors.

Customer Discovery

Before building the program, I focused on understanding the target user. I conducted 200+ conversations and interviews with college students to understand their relationship with travel, perceptions of travel advising, motivations for joining a program like Fora, and barriers that might prevent participation.

The research helped answer questions including:

  • Do students understand what a travel advisor does?
  • What would motivate a student to become an advisor?
  • What prevents students from seeing travel advising as relevant to them?
  • How should the opportunity be positioned?
  • What information does a student need before deciding to join?
  • What would make the program feel credible, exciting, and achievable?

These conversations shifted ForaU from a broad student acquisition idea into a more clearly defined product proposition centered around entrepreneurship, travel, flexibility, and earning potential.

Product Strategy

Customer discovery informed several core requirements for the ForaU experience.

Accessible
Students needed to immediately understand that prior travel-industry experience was not required.
Entrepreneurial
The program needed to feel like an opportunity to build something of their own rather than simply take on another campus job.
Flexible
The experience needed to fit within a student's existing academic and social schedule.
Tangible
The value proposition needed to clearly communicate how students could turn travel recommendations and existing networks into real bookings.
Community-Oriented
A campus-based model could create both social proof and a network of students learning from one another.

Key Learnings

This experience taught me the reality behind entering new markets, and how it isn't just about adjusting a product, but also the approach to the consumer. The most important part of the process was understanding why a college student would want the product in the first place. The project then became a translation for a business model.

Innovation & Entrepreneurship

ForaU represents my broader approach to innovation and entrepreneurship: identifying an opportunity, understanding the customer deeply, building the smallest version of a solution to test the hypothesis, and using real-world feedback to determine what comes next.

Overview

Enterprise Entertainment is a student-run entertainment business within Campus Enterprises at Duke University focused on creating and executing large-scale social experiences for the Duke community.

My work within EE was centered on a key question: how could we approach campus entertainment as a business and a product rather than simply a series of individual events?

I wanted to come into this experience less like a mere business owner and more like a product manager, hoping to scale using product strategy, business development, marketing, event design, and growth cross-functionally rather than singling out approaches.

What came from this question was a new experience that created a marketplace between students and external vendors, where we acted as the primary liaison. Through this we offered well priced and well executed events for students, and consistent revenue for vendors.

The Opportunity

The college entertainment market is definitely an unusual product challenge. Students have a large desire for social experiences, but their preferences change quickly, and they have countless competing options for their time and attention. It's not as easy to bet on a well-planned event as it may be for a well-developed product.

Creating a successful event therefore requires more than securing a venue and selling tickets. The entire experience has to resonate early and resonate well with the target audience.

Reframing the Question

Rather than asking "what event should we throw?" I wanted us to ask: "what experience do students want, and how can we build a repeatable business around delivering it?"

Understanding the Customer

Our primary customer was the Duke student. Because Enterprise Entertainment operated directly within the community it served, we had a close feedback loop with our users. Student behavior, conversations, ticket sales, social engagement, and event attendance all provided signals about demand.

Product Strategy

I approached each event as its own product. Every event had a target customer, value proposition, brand, acquisition strategy, pricing model, operational requirements, and measures of success.

Student-Led
Events needed to reflect what students actually wanted, not what an outside entertainment company assumed they wanted.
Distinctive
Each event needed a recognizable concept and identity that gave students a reason to choose it over competing social options.
Shareable
Strong concepts and branding could turn attendees into a distribution channel through word-of-mouth and social media.
Financially Viable
Creative decisions had to exist alongside real constraints around ticket pricing, venue costs, talent, marketing, and operating expenses.
Repeatable
The goal was not simply to produce one successful event. We wanted to understand what worked well enough to reproduce and scale it.

Growth Strategy

One of my primary areas of focus was increasing awareness and engagement with Enterprise Entertainment. Our growth strategy centered on creating deals and pricing models that no other group could compete with. As we began to place ourselves as the primary event planner on campus, we had the best pricing and efficiency in the market, and there was no way to compete with that.

We also built strong relationships with the venues to the point they only wanted to work with us, since we understood the venue and its nuances, and repeatable events were incredibly simple since they did not need to deal with new user friction.

Business Model & Operations

Enterprise Entertainment also required translating creative ideas into financially sustainable events. The smartest choice we made was contracting venues into our client portfolio with premium pricing. Through this, we had a consistent revenue stream, client trust, and the lowest pricing available on the market.

Innovation & Entrepreneurship

Enterprise Entertainment expanded my understanding of what entrepreneurship can look like. Innovation meant recognizing an opportunity within an existing market and developing a better way to serve it.

Our competitive advantage was proximity to our customer. Because we were students building experiences for students, we could quickly identify emerging preferences and test ideas with real validation.
